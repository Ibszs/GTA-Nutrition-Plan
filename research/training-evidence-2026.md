# Training evidence for the 2026 program review

Reviewed September 7, 2026. This is a targeted evidence record for a practical training-plan decision. It is not an individualized medical assessment, a pharmacology guide, or proof that any particular routine will produce a guaranteed physique outcome.

## Planning context and evidence boundaries

The current planning context is a 21-year-old lifter, 6 ft 3 in, with approximately one year of resistance-training experience, a self-described informed approach to training, combined muscle and strength goals, and weekday work from about 7:30 a.m. to 4:00 p.m. A commercial gym is assumed for planning. Earlier records mention approximately 170 lb and no reported injury, but those details were not freshly confirmed for this review. Current health status, laboratory data, medication use, sleep, recovery capacity, recent training consistency, and exact performance numbers remain unknown.

The repository's existing program is a four-session upper/lower routine. The evidence below is drawn mainly from healthy adults who were not using supraphysiologic androgens. That population is the correct baseline for selecting exercise organization and managing fatigue; it does not establish that a person using pharmacologic androgen exposure should tolerate more work.

## Volume, frequency, split, effort, progression, and recovery

| Question | Source and population | Intervention and result | Limitation and application |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| What does the current professional synthesis conclude? | [Currier et al. for the American College of Sports Medicine, 2026](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/41843416/), overview of 137 systematic reviews representing more than 30,000 healthy adults aged 18 or older; included interventions lasted 6–52 weeks. The [ACSM announcement](https://acsm.org/resistance-training-guidelines-update-2026/) provides the public summary. | Progressive resistance training improved strength, size, power, and function. Heavier loading, 2–3 sets, and at least two weekly sessions supported strength; higher weekly volume (about 10 or more sets) supported hypertrophy. Failure, equipment type, and complex periodization did not consistently change outcomes. | This is an overview of reviews rather than one controlled routine, and its population is healthy adults without a specific supraphysiologic-androgen subgroup. |
| Does more weekly volume help? | [Schoenfeld, Ogborn, and Krieger, 2017](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/27433992/), systematic review/meta-analysis of 15 studies and 34 treatment groups. | Weekly resistance-training volume showed a graded relationship with muscle-size outcomes; the analysis estimated a small positive association per additional set. | The studies used mixed populations, durations, and hypertrophy measures. It supports gradual volume decisions, not a universal optimum. |
| What does newer dose-response modelling add? | [Pelland et al., 2025 online/2026 publication record](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/41343037/), 67 studies and 2,058 participants, 79.1% male, mean age about 25 years. | Direct and indirect sets were modelled as total, fractional, or direct. Fractional counting fit best. Volume was positively associated with hypertrophy and strength, with diminishing returns; frequency had little identifiable independent effect on hypertrophy but a positive, diminishing relationship with strength. | Meta-regression cannot establish an individual optimum. Most interventions were short and were not performed by enhanced lifters. Use fractional sets as a transparent planning convention only. |
| Does frequency matter when weekly volume is matched? | [Schoenfeld, Grgic, and Krieger, 2019](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30558493/), systematic review/meta-analysis of 25 studies, including trained and untrained subgroups. | Higher and lower frequencies did not meaningfully differ for hypertrophy when volume was equated. Non-equated studies modestly favored higher frequency. | Frequency remains useful for distributing per-session fatigue, fitting work, and improving exercise quality. It is not a hypertrophy advantage by itself. |
| Is a split better than full body? | [Ramos-Campo et al., 2024](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/38595233/), systematic review/meta-analysis of 14 studies and 392 healthy adults. | Split and full-body routines produced similar strength and hypertrophy when volume was equated. | The studies were generally short and did not test supraphysiologic androgen users. Choose the schedule that supports consistent, high-quality work. |
| Which load/set/frequency combinations support strength and hypertrophy? | [Currier et al., 2023](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/37414459/), Bayesian network meta-analysis: 178 strength studies/5,097 participants and 119 hypertrophy studies/3,364 participants; 45% of participants were women. | All resistance-training prescriptions outperformed no exercise. Higher loads above 80% 1RM ranked best for strength; multiset training ranked strongly for hypertrophy, with twice-weekly exposure among the highest-ranked hypertrophy conditions. | Network rankings are indirect and do not validate a particular split. Use heavy first lifts for strength and multiple moderate-repetition sets for muscle growth. |
| Does periodization help? | [Moesgaard et al., 2022](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/35044672/), systematic review/meta-analysis of 35 volume-equated studies. | Periodized training modestly favored 1RM strength over non-periodized training. Undulating periodization favored strength in trained subgroups; hypertrophy did not differ reliably. | The periodization models varied. A simple strength/hypertrophy alternation is a coaching choice, not evidence that complex cycles are necessary. |
| Must sets reach failure? | [Refalo et al., 2023](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/36334240/), systematic review/meta-analysis of 15 studies in healthy adults. | Set-failure definitions were inconsistent. There was no clear advantage for momentary failure over non-failure for hypertrophy. | The evidence supports close, repeatable effort without requiring failure on every set. RIR accuracy varies by exercise and experience. |
| What does a controlled RIR trial show? | [Refalo et al., 2024](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/38393985/), 18 resistance-trained adults (12 men and 6 women), aged 18–40 with at least three years of training, eight weeks. | One leg performed leg press and extension to failure; the other used approximately 2 RIR on leg press and 1 RIR on extension. Quadriceps thickness and volume were similar; failure caused greater velocity and repetition loss. | Small sample, quadriceps-only, short duration, and RIR estimates were not laboratory measurements. Start most work at about 1–3 RIR. |
| Does the RIR relationship differ for strength and size? | [Robinson et al., 2024](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/38970765/), exploratory meta-regression of 55 studies, with 243 strength effects and 140 hypertrophy effects from 26 studies. | Strength showed no clear dose-response advantage from closer failure. Hypertrophy estimates tended to improve as sets ended closer to failure. | RIR was estimated from study descriptions and model fit was modest. It supports using close but sustainable effort, not a precise failure prescription. |
| Can progression use reps as well as load? | [Plotkin et al., 2022](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9528903/), 43 trained adults (27 men and 16 women; mean age 23.1 years; at least one year of training), eight weeks. | Participants performed four lower-body exercises twice weekly. One group progressed load while holding the rep range; the other progressed repetitions at a fixed load. Hypertrophy was broadly similar and strength differences were small. | Lower-body-focused, short, and 38 participants completed the study. Double progression is a reasonable practical method; heavy loading remains useful for strength specificity. |
| How much rest is useful? | [Schoenfeld et al., 2016](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/26605807/), 21 young resistance-trained men, eight weeks. | Three-minute rests were compared with one-minute rests during three full-body sessions per week. Longer rests produced greater squat and bench strength and greater anterior-thigh thickness. | Small sample and one fixed program. Rest should preserve performance rather than obey an arbitrary timer. |
| What does the newer rest meta-analysis show? | [Singer et al., 2024](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/39205815/), Bayesian review of nine studies and 19 muscle-size measurements in healthy adults. | Resting more than 60 seconds may provide a small hypertrophy benefit; no appreciable hypertrophy difference was detected beyond roughly 90 seconds. | Hypertrophy estimates were heterogeneous. Strength-focused compounds commonly require longer rests to preserve load and repetitions. |
| Does a planned complete break improve growth? | [Coleman et al., 2024](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/38274324/), 39 trained young adults (29 men and 10 women), nine-week high-volume program with a one-week midpoint cessation. | Complete cessation did not improve lower-body hypertrophy and negatively affected lower-body strength relative to continuous training. | This was not a deload-volume comparison and did not involve androgen users. A reduced-volume week is a fatigue-management option, not a proven growth boost. |

The resulting coaching choices are to use approximately 3–4 minutes for heavy primary lifts, 2–3 minutes for secondary compounds, and roughly 1–2 minutes for isolation work; keep most sets at 1–3 RIR; progress repetitions and then load within a stable rep range; and adjust volume only after observing several exposures of actual performance, sleep, soreness, and motivation.

## Testosterone-related evidence and its limits

The training literature does not contain a direct trial comparing upper/lower, push/pull/legs, or an eight-day split in healthy recreational lifters using supraphysiologic testosterone. The following studies establish the population distinction without supplying a training prescription:

| Population | Source and participants | Intervention/result | Why it does not answer the split question |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Healthy young men with pharmacologic androgen exposure | [Bhasin et al., 1996](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/8637535/), 43 normal men, randomized to placebo/testosterone and exercise/no exercise groups. | A short controlled androgen intervention produced substantially greater fat-free mass, muscle-size, and strength changes in the androgen-plus-exercise condition. | No comparison of weekly set volume, frequency, split, or long-term recovery. It demonstrates an anabolic effect, not a higher-volume rule. |
| Healthy eugonadal young men | [Bhasin et al., 2001](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/11701431/), 61 men aged 18–35 after endogenous suppression, followed for 20 weeks under graded androgen exposure. | Fat-free mass, muscle volume, strength, and power changed in relation to circulating androgen exposure. | Endocrine dose-response design, not a real-world training-split trial; endogenous suppression also limits direct application. |
| Healthy exercising adults across AAS trials | [Andrews et al., 2018](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/29994823/), meta-analysis of 25 randomized placebo-controlled studies; 21 strength and 14 lean-mass comparisons. | AAS exposure was associated with a moderate lean-mass advantage and a smaller strength advantage. Only 13 studies reported adverse effects, and study durations and exposures varied. | The review pooled different agents and protocols and did not identify an optimal training organization. |
| Frail older hypogonadal men | [Sullivan et al., 2005](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/16260965/), 71 men, mean age 78.2 years, resistance training and testosterone/placebo for 12 weeks. | High-intensity resistance training improved strength; androgen treatment increased thigh muscle area, but there was no significant exercise-by-treatment interaction. | Frailty, age, hypogonadism, and clinical treatment context differ substantially from a healthy young recreational lifter. |
| Older men with low-normal testosterone | [Hildreth et al., 2013](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/23533227/), 167 community-dwelling men, mean age 66, baseline total testosterone 200–350 ng/dL, testosterone gel/placebo with or without progressive resistance training for 12 months. | In the training group, testosterone improved fat-free mass but did not add a clear functional or strength benefit over placebo. | This is replacement-oriented evidence in an older, low-testosterone population, not evidence for supraphysiologic use in young lifters. |
| Recreational strength athletes using AAS in usual practice | [Hartgens et al.](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/20086552/), prospective unblinded observational study of 19 self-administering users and 16 controls. | Self-administered exposure was associated with short-term bodyweight and lean-mass changes. | Training, compounds, exposures, and adherence were not standardized; no split or volume inference is valid. |

The conservative conclusion is that androgen exposure may increase the capacity to gain mass and strength, but it does not identify the amount of training that should be added, guarantee faster recovery, or remove the need to monitor performance and fatigue. This record contains no dosing, diagnosis, or cessation advice.

## Selected program scaffold

The selected default is a preference-led five-session, seven-day sequence:

1. Upper
2. Lower
3. Rest
4. Chest + Back
5. Legs
6. Shoulders + Arms
7. Rest

This gives five gym days in a calendar week. Chest, back, and lower body each have two planned direct regional exposures: Upper plus Chest + Back for chest and back, and Lower plus Legs for lower body. With similarly timed sessions, those pairs are about 72 hours apart within the week and about 96 hours apart across the Sunday-to-Monday boundary. The sequence is a practical response to the stated five-day preference, not evidence that five sessions, these day placements, or these intervals are biologically optimal.

Arms and delts should be marked as direct only when isolation or other target-specific work is assigned on Upper and/or Shoulders + Arms. Pressing and pulling also create indirect triceps, front-delt, and biceps exposure, so their direct frequency cannot be inferred from the calendar alone. The Thursday Chest + Back session is followed by Friday Legs; one leg day therefore sits between that upper session and the next Monday Upper session. Shoulders + Arms on Saturday is followed by Upper on Monday, which is about 48 hours with similarly timed sessions. These are scheduling intervals, not guarantees of local or systemic recovery.

Strength work should place the main press, squat, hinge, or pull early, use heavier loads and longer rest, and leave repeatable repetitions in reserve. Hypertrophy work can use moderate loads, stable exercises, controlled range of motion, and closer effort on selected safer movements. The first implementation should redistribute the current program's demonstrated working-set dose across these five sessions rather than automatically add a training day or increase volume.

The implemented five-day program totals 69 nominal working sets per week:

| Session | Implemented work | Nominal direct-set roll-up |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Upper (12 sets) | Flat barbell press 3 × 5–8; dumbbell row 3 × 6–10; incline dumbbell press 2 × 8–12; neutral-grip pulldown 2 × 8–12; dumbbell lateral raise 2 × 12–20. | Chest 5; back 5; side delt 2. |
| Lower (13 sets) | Hack squat 3 × 5–8; barbell Romanian deadlift 3 × 6–8; seated leg curl 2 × 8–12; standing calf raise 3 × 8–12; cable crunch 2 × 10–15. | Quads 3; hamstrings 5; calves 3; abs 2. |
| Chest + Back (11 sets) | Incline dumbbell press 3 × 8–12; seated row 3 × 8–12; cable fly 2 × 10–15; neutral-grip pulldown 3 × 10–15. | Chest 5; back 6. |
| Legs (15 sets) | Sled press 3 × 10–15; dumbbell rear-foot-elevated split squat 2 × 8–12; seated leg curl 3 × 10–15; leg extension 2 × 12–20; standing calf raise 3 × 12–20; cable crunch 2 × 10–15. | Quads 7; hamstrings 3; calves 3; abs 2. |
| Shoulders + Arms (18 sets) | Dumbbell shoulder press 2 × 6–10; dumbbell lateral raise 3 × 12–20; rear-pec-deck raise 3 × 12–20; dumbbell curl 3 × 8–12; overhead rope extension 3 × 10–15; hammer curl 2 × 10–15; rope pushdown 2 × 10–15. | Overhead press 2; side delt 3; rear delt 3; biceps 5; triceps 5. |

Across the week, the nominal direct totals are chest 10, back 11, quads 10, hamstrings 8, calves 6, abs 4, side delts 5, rear delts 3, overhead press 2, biceps 5, and triceps 5. Hack squats, Romanian deadlifts, sled presses, and split squats also involve the glutes, but their overlapping contribution is not presented as a separate direct-set total. These counts are a bookkeeping baseline: pressing and pulling add indirect arm and delt exposure, and the fractional-set convention remains an estimate rather than a universal physiological conversion. The ledger is one fewer nominal set than the prior four-session upper/lower baseline of 70 nominal working sets, so it redistributes work without adding volume. It should be judged after several real exposures; the evidence does not require completing a fixed 16-week block before making a fatigue or progression adjustment.

A practical starting effort target is about 2 RIR on usual work and about 3 RIR while baselining a new setup. An occasional lighter session with about half the usual number of sets at about 4 RIR can be used for fatigue management; this is a reduced-volume session, not half of a repetition or a normal hard-set dose. About 3 minutes for heavy compounds, 2–3 minutes for secondary compounds, and 90–120 seconds for isolation work are compatible with the evidence summary; readiness and performance should determine whether an interval is extended.

Direct and indirect exposure should be represented separately. A press can count as one direct chest set and one indirect triceps/front-delt exposure; for planning, an indirect exposure may be displayed as 0.5 fractional set. The app should label that as a bookkeeping convention, since the literature does not prove a universal conversion. For a target receiving `a` direct sets on Upper and `b` direct sets on Chest + Back, direct cycle volume is `a + b`; indirect volume is calculated separately from the relevant compound work. The same calculation applies to lower-body overlap between squat, hinge, press, split-squat, curl, and extension patterns.

An eight-day sequence with six lifting days averages 5.25 sessions per seven-day equivalent. A fixed seven-day upper/lower schedule with four lifting days averages four sessions, while a six-session ten-day hybrid averages 4.2. None has demonstrated superiority when effective volume is matched. The five-day sequence should be judged by adherence, performance, fatigue, and progression, and changed only from those observations; a higher-frequency eight-day option remains a later experiment rather than an assumed upgrade.

## Cycle boundaries and recovery evidence gap

No included study tested a pharmacology-specific cycle boundary or a post-cycle training prescription. The app should therefore avoid generating one. A normal fatigue rule can remain available: if performance declines across repeated exposures alongside worsening sleep, soreness, or motivation, reduce working-set volume temporarily and keep technique and effort controlled; resume normal work after performance stabilizes. This is a general training-management choice, not a claim about endocrine recovery.

## Independent comparison

An independent parallel assessment reached the same main decision: split selection is secondary to sustainable volume, exercise quality, effort, progression, and recovery. A four-day upper/lower routine, the former six-day upper/lower/rest rotation, or this five-day calendar can each be reasonable when matched for effective work and supported by adherence. The five-day sequence is selected here because it fits the stated preference and redistributes existing work; that choice does not establish superiority over the other schedules. The assessments differed in emphasis, with this record giving more space to fractional-set accounting and the distinction between supraphysiologic healthy-young-men evidence and older TRT evidence. They are complementary assessments; neither establishes that one model is inherently superior.

## Claim classification

- **Study-supported:** volume has a positive but diminishing relationship with adaptation; frequency has little independent hypertrophy effect when volume is matched; split and full-body routines are similar when volume is matched; heavy loading helps strength; failure is not required; longer rests preserve performance; load and repetition progression can both work.
- **Coaching choices:** the five-session seven-day sequence, redistributing existing working sets across those sessions, the strength and hypertrophy exercise emphasis, using about 2 RIR in usual work with approximately 3 RIR for initial baselining and occasional lighter sessions with about half the usual sets at 4 RIR, and separating direct from fractional indirect set counts. The 72-hour, 96-hour, and approximately 48-hour figures are calendar arithmetic for this layout, not recovery prescriptions.
- **Not established:** that five sessions or this layout is optimal, that arms or delts receive two direct exposures without explicit exercise assignment, an enhanced-lifter optimal split, an enhanced-lifter optimal volume, faster recovery sufficient to justify high volume, a special cycle-boundary prescription, a guaranteed rate of muscle gain, or a guaranteed body shape.
